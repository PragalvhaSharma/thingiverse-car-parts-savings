Statistics –
How this works from a Thing to a dollar figure, and what your clicks do

The pipeline. Each row is a printable car part published on Thingiverse. The pipeline turned it into a part query, searched eBay’s sold listings, and a strict two-stage matcher (candidate matching, then a photos-both-sides judge) kept a listing only when it shows the same physical part — that pass counted 56 of 110 rows. A counted row’s savings are (item price − print cost) × downloads, clamped at zero, banked as repair (spare parts) or upgrade (accessories) by its classification. Prices are the item only — no shipping (the dashboard reports the with-shipping sensitivity separately).

Counted rows — auto-confirmed. The strict matcher already verified these, so they arrive confirmed and need nothing from you. Veto ✕ kicks one out and its dollars leave the totals immediately. Expand the row to see the printed part next to the listing photo, Confirm it as human-verified, or swap the model’s listing for another candidate (or one entered by hand) — the row re-prices at your pick.

Uncounted rows. The strict pass refused these, so they currently add $0. The detail shows the candidate pool the matcher saw — click Same part ✓ on the listing that really is this part to price the row with it, or Veto if nothing sold matches. “Enter it by hand” covers listings found elsewhere on eBay.

Bookkeeping. Every click saves to the server, so all reviewers see the same state; Z undoes. JSON ⇩ downloads the decisions and the statistics, and apply_review.py folds them into a reviewed CSV (never touching the original) that the dashboard rebuilds from. Report ⇩ downloads a standalone HTML write-up of the same review — totals, what review changed, and every row you moved with both photos — meant for sending to someone rather than for the pipeline. Photos load through an image proxy — Thingiverse blocks hotlinks, so a blank thumbnail is the listing’s problem, not a wrong match.
